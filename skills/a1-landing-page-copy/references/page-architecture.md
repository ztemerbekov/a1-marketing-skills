# Page Architecture

The page is an evaluation path, not a fixed stack of conversion formulas. Use the smallest set of blocks that answers this reader's buying questions.

## 1. Orientation

The opening should let the reader identify:

- what the offer is in a familiar category;
- whether it is meant for their situation;
- the most important supported outcome or use;
- the next action available now;
- a critical price, date, format, or eligibility condition when it drives fit.

Lead with the offer and reader relevance rather than company history, mission, or a slogan that could describe any product.

## 2. Value in Use

Develop the proposition through a small number of high-value reader situations. Each block should connect:

1. a reader task, concern, or decision;
2. a product feature, mechanism, service step, or policy;
3. the supported consequence for the reader;
4. evidence or a demonstration.

Use examples close to the primary audience. Do not force every feature onto the page; secondary capabilities can move to specifications or FAQs.

## 3. Evidence-Bearing Visuals

Decide what should be shown before writing a long explanation. Useful evidence includes:

- the product in the relevant setting;
- a screenshot or workflow;
- a before-and-after state with comparable conditions;
- a sample deliverable;
- a diagram of a mechanism or process;
- a table of plans, specifications, or fit criteria;
- a sourced result or case artifact;
- a policy, guarantee, or implementation timeline.

Write a content note that tells production what the reader must learn from the visual. Avoid decorative stock imagery, abstract icons, and generic team photographs as substitutes for proof.

Captions should add context, conditions, or a conclusion rather than restating what is visible.

## 4. Detail, Fit, and Objections

After the main value case, provide the information needed for an informed choice:

- package or service scope;
- how delivery or onboarding works;
- time, technical, location, or eligibility requirements;
- price or the supported method for obtaining it;
- guarantees, cancellation, returns, or support conditions;
- comparisons among actual plans or alternatives;
- who the offer suits and who should choose another route;
- answers to real recurring questions.

Keep short answers visible. Use disclosure controls only for genuinely long reference detail.

Testimonials are useful when they reveal a buyer situation, usage, result, limitation, or fit judgment. Pure praise adds little decision value.

## 5. Transaction

The transaction block should restate the offer in operational terms:

- what the reader receives;
- price or pricing process;
- important terms and limits;
- the exact action;
- what information the reader must provide;
- what happens immediately after the action;
- response or delivery timing when supplied.

Use a CTA that names the real step: `Start a trial`, `See availability`, `Request the three plans`, or `Register for the workshop`. Do not promise a low-commitment action when the next screen asks for something materially different.

## Page Length

Length follows unanswered buying questions. Keep a block when it adds a distinct fact, proof item, condition, comparison, or action. Remove it when it repeats the proposition, praises the company, or fills a conventional landing-page slot without helping the decision.

If materially different audiences need different outcomes, examples, proof, or terms, recommend separate page variants rather than blending incompatible arguments.
