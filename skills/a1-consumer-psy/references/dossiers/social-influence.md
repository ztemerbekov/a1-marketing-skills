# Social-influence model dossiers

These are separate diagnostic tools, not interchangeable ways to say “social proof.” A popularity claim, approval pressure, a private update from evidence, source credibility, exchange, identity, and scarcity require different facts and make different predictions. Each model section owns its evidence grade, sources, and research limitation.

## Truthful social-evidence minimum

Before a claim about other people is displayed or tested, identify its **reference group**, **source/provenance**, **baseline** (count, denominator, or rate), **time window**, **public or private context**, and **outcome** (for example, completed purchase rather than intention). Preserve those facts in the claim. If one is unknown, do not imply it; use neutral copy or obtain the evidence. Do not call a generic “most popular,” a star badge, or a large number social evidence without those facts.

<a id="n11"></a>

## N11 — Social Identity

- **Mechanism:** self-definition through a meaningful group membership can affect group-contingent comparison, norms, and behavior.
- **Diagnostic signals:** self-identification or a valid categorization is present; the group is meaningful and relevant; behavior differs in the predicted in-group/out-group direction.
- **Distinguish from:** demographic targeting, similarity, generic popularity, descriptive or injunctive norms, normative pressure, and an authority claim. Group membership is neither consent to persuasion nor proof that all members share a preference.
- **Useful test:** compare a truthful, voluntary identity-relevant message with neutral copy; measure self-identification and private behavior. Track the target action plus exclusion, stereotyping, polarization, subgroup disparity, trust, and access after declining.
- **Falsifier:** the label changes behavior without identification, or no predicted group-contingent pattern appears.
- **Applicability limit:** group meaning, interdependence, status, boundary permeability, and context moderate the effect; do not infer a sensitive identity or manufacture a community.

### Evidence (B)

- Oyserman (2009), *Identity-based motivation*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013740).
- Reed et al. (2012), identity-based consumer behavior framework, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijresmar.2012.08.002).
- Balliet, Wu & De Dreu (2014), in-group cooperation meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037737).

The cooperation synthesis supports a group-contingent behavioral pattern, not a unique identity mechanism in a local marketing outcome. Group meaning, interdependence, status, and boundary permeability matter; do not infer or manufacture identity.

<a id="m21"></a>

## M21 — Bandwagon cues / descriptive norms

- **Mechanism:** truthful information that a relevant group performs an action can change perceived prevalence and choice. A **descriptive norm** says what the group does; an **injunctive norm** says what it approves. “Social proof” is only a practitioner umbrella, never a diagnosis.
- **Diagnostic signals:** a truthful, relevant reference group; an auditable baseline and time window; a clear descriptive versus injunctive cue; and a public/private context. The displayed outcome must match the claim.
- **Distinguish from:** N12 approval/sanction motive, N13 private evidence-based belief update, N11 identity, M24 expertise, M22 exchange, M27 scarcity, and a mere review count. Popularity does not establish quality, approval, independence, or correctness.
- **Useful test:** compare an auditable descriptive cue with neutral copy, stratified by baseline behavior where relevant. Measure perceived norm and the stated outcome; track boomerang among already-high performers, manipulation perception, trust, cancellation, and minority harm.
- **Falsifier:** the cue changes neither perceived prevalence nor the target outcome, or reliably worsens the net outcome.
- **Applicability limit:** group relevance, source, cost, culture, baseline behavior, and public/private setting vary the result. A descriptive norm can boomerang; it is not a universal conversion lever.

### Evidence (B)

- Schultz et al. (2007), descriptive-norm boomerang field experiment, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01917.x).
- Melnyk, van Herpen & Trijp (2013), consumer social-norm meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1027/2151-2604/a000352).
- Wang, Chu & Huang (2023), bandwagon-cue credibility meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/00936502221124395).
- Papakonstantinou et al. (2025), social-norms messaging review and meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02275-6).

Effects vary by norm type, reference group/source, baseline behavior, cost, culture, and public/private context. The health synthesis was strongly publication-bias-sensitive; a generic popularity claim is neither a local forecast nor evidence of quality or approval.

<a id="m22"></a>

## M22 — Reciprocity Principle

- **Mechanism:** a real, intentional, unconditional benefit from a source can change the probability of a later reciprocal action.
- **Diagnostic signals:** the same source provides a useful benefit before the request, with no concealed purchase condition; timing, benefit value, baseline action rate, and actual response are known.
- **Distinguish from:** loyalty, discounting, a reciprocal concession, commitment, liking, gratitude, and an operational change that merely removes friction or shifts a benefit mechanically. A gift effect alone does not identify a debt norm rather than gratitude or liking.
- **Useful test:** randomize a useful unconditional benefit before the request versus the same benefit after it and no benefit. Keep request, audience, and timing otherwise equal; measure completed action and trust, unsubscribe, complaints, repeat action, margin, and vulnerable-segment outcomes.
- **Falsifier:** no useful behavioral difference, or the difference disappears when plausible gratitude/liking alternatives are examined.
- **Applicability limit:** effects are context- and timing-dependent; a fundraising or lab result is not a retention forecast. Never create hidden debt, attach an undisclosed price, or make refusal costly.

### Evidence (B)

- Gouldner (1960), *The Norm of Reciprocity*, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/2092623).
- Falk (2007), gift-exchange field experiment, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0262.2007.00800.x).
- Chuan, Kessler & Milkman (2018), timing of reciprocity in charitable giving, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1708293115).

There is no single modern meta-analysis of direct reciprocity in persuasion. Favor, gift exchange, reciprocal concession, gratitude, liking, and a hidden obligation are not interchangeable; timing and the actual behavioral outcome limit transportability.

<a id="m24"></a>

## M24 — Authority / source credibility

- **Mechanism:** with message content held constant, truthful domain-relevant **expertise**, **trustworthiness**, or a disclosed **legitimate power** role can change judgment or action.
- **Diagnostic signals:** verified, domain-relevant credentials; a source and conflicts disclosed; identical evidence/content; and a source effect on an accuracy-sensitive or behavioral outcome.
- **Distinguish from:** social status or fame, celebrity endorsement, liking, popularity, obedience, and an authority costume. Expertise, trustworthiness, legitimate power, social status, and obedience are separate variables: formal rank must not substitute for evidence or meaningful choice.
- **Useful test:** randomize verified credential disclosure versus a neutral source around identical evidence, then measure accuracy-sensitive action or completed behavior. Track calibrated trust, errors, complaints, delayed or repeated action, and harm to vulnerable users.
- **Falsifier:** no source effect after blinded content-quality control, or an equally strong effect from irrelevant/false credentials.
- **Applicability limit:** findings are heterogeneous and weaker for delayed or repeated behavior; conflicts, counterarguments, and irrelevant expertise can reverse the effect. High-stakes decisions need independent evidence, not rank alone.

### Evidence (B)

- Hamer et al. (2026), credible-source behavioral systematic review and meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672261445474).
- Wilson & Sherrell (1993), source-effects meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/009207039302100202).
- Ismagilova et al. (2020), source-credibility characteristics and consumer behavior meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretconser.2019.01.005).
- Knoll & Matthes (2017), celebrity-endorsement meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11747-016-0503-8).

The 2026 review reports a small heterogeneous behavioral effect and weaker evidence for delayed or repeated behavior. Expertise, trustworthiness, legitimate power/status, obedience, fame, and message quality must remain separate; source relevance and conflict disclosure are necessary local conditions.

<a id="m27"></a>

## M27 — Scarcity cues

- **Mechanism:** a truthful cue about objectively limited availability can affect valuation or urgency.
- **Diagnostic signals:** real stock, capacity, deadline, or eligibility limit; its source and audit trail; the actual baseline availability; and a claim that is removed when the constraint ends.
- **Distinguish from:** popularity/demand, bandwagon cues, loss framing, uniqueness, price promotion, and fake urgency. Do not replace one scarcity source with another or infer real scarcity from high interest.
- **Useful test:** only with a real limit, compare transparent source-specific disclosure with neutral availability without changing stock. Measure incremental **completed purchase**, not clicks or purchase intention; track margin, returns/cancellations, complaints, trust, repeat purchase, and vulnerable-segment outcomes.
- **Falsifier:** no practically useful net completed-purchase change, or gains are offset by returns, cancellation, or lost trust.
- **Applicability limit:** outcomes vary by scarcity source, product, involvement, sincerity, and horizon. Evidence often relies on intentions or proxy decisions; repeated timers and high-risk purchases can backfire.

### Evidence (B)

- Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022), product-scarcity meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2022.06.003).
- Ladeira et al. (2023), product-scarcity meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/mar.21816).

Scarcity effects are heterogeneous by source of scarcity, product, and outcome. The literature often uses intention or decision proxies; do not turn it into a completed-purchase forecast, and never fabricate stock or a deadline.

<a id="n12"></a>

## N12 — Normative Social Influence

- **Mechanism:** a person conforms publicly to obtain approval or avoid disapproval or sanctions.
- **Diagnostic signals:** behavior is more sensitive to public visibility, relevant-group approval, or sanctions than to private choice; private belief is measured separately.
- **Distinguish from:** an injunctive-norm message (which may not activate the motive), M21 descriptive prevalence, N13 information about reality, and general compliance. A public choice is not proof of private acceptance.
- **Useful test:** vary a truthful approval cue and public/private response while holding evidence constant; measure actual public action and private belief independently. Track shame, reactance, dissent suppression, false consensus, complaints, and subgroup harm.
- **Falsifier:** private belief changes equally without sensitivity to approval or public visibility.
- **Applicability limit:** observability, sanctions, reference-group relevance, culture, and reactance matter. Do not shame, punish dissent, or turn an optional decision into a public commitment.

### Evidence (B)

- Deutsch & Gerard (1955), normative versus informational social influence, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046408).
- Bond & Smith (1996), Asch-paradigm conformity meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.119.1.111).
- Melnyk, van Herpen & Trijp (2013), consumer social-norm meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1027/2151-2604/a000352).

An injunctive message is not proof of an approval/sanction motive. Evidence varies with public visibility, sanctions, group relevance, culture, and reactance; public compliance must be separated from private belief.

<a id="n13"></a>

## N13 — Informational Social Influence

- **Mechanism:** people use others’ judgments or behavior as evidence about reality or the correct action, especially under uncertainty.
- **Diagnostic signals:** a private belief or accuracy-sensitive choice moves with truthful, diagnostic, independent social evidence; source sample, independence, and uncertainty are known.
- **Distinguish from:** M21 descriptive prevalence, N12 approval pressure, M24 source expertise, and generic popularity. Observed prevalence can activate several mechanisms; it is not informational influence by itself.
- **Useful test:** vary uncertainty and the diagnosticity/independence of a truthful source while holding approval constant. Measure private belief calibration and behavior; track herding, cascades, false consensus, minority suppression, trust, and error.
- **Falsifier:** the response is public-only, or it persists when the source is known dependent or uninformative.
- **Applicability limit:** source dependence and correlated reviews can make a large count uninformative. High-stakes claims need provenance and independent evidence; correct a stale or false cue promptly.

### Evidence (B)

- Deutsch & Gerard (1955), normative versus informational social influence, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046408).
- Melnyk, van Herpen & Trijp (2013), consumer social-norm meta-analysis, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1027/2151-2604/a000352).

The foundational distinction is strong, but a dedicated modern pooled estimate for private informational updating is not established here. Source independence, diagnosticity, uncertainty, and the distinction between belief and public response limit transportability; correlated or unproven reviews are not independent evidence.
