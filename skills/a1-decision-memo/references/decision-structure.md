# Decision Structure

A decision memo is layered rather than merely short. It gives the conclusion and request first, then preserves enough detail for verification.

## The Executive Layer

The opening should answer, as applicable:

- What is the current status?
- What changed or created the issue?
- Why does it matter now?
- What decision, approval, response, or awareness is requested?
- By when?
- What does the source owner recommend, if anything?

Use a compact paragraph or three to six bullets. Do not force every memo into bullets when one direct paragraph is clearer.

Put any request beside the summary rather than hiding it at the end. When the memo is for awareness only, state explicitly that no action is required.

## Body Sections

Choose headings from the reader's questions and the source material. Common sections include:

- Current status
- Decision requested
- Recommendation
- Options and tradeoffs
- Impact
- Risks and unknowns
- Budget or resources
- Timeline and dependencies
- Background or chronology
- Next steps
- Appendix or source detail

These are options, not a mandatory template.

## Conclusion First

Open each section with the point the reader needs, then explain how the situation developed.

Weak order:

1. nine months of history;
2. process detail;
3. the project has stopped.

Decision order:

1. the project is stopped and the current consequence;
2. the required response;
3. the history that explains why.

Chronology remains available; it no longer controls the reading path.

## Options and Tradeoffs

When the sources contain alternatives, compare them on stable, supplied dimensions such as cost, time, risk, reversibility, expected effect, dependency, or opportunity cost.

Do not create artificial symmetry. If evidence is stronger for one option, show that. If the choosing criterion is missing, state it as an unresolved decision rather than selecting by editorial instinct.

## Detail Without Obstruction

Keep technical, legal, financial, or operational detail when it may affect the decision. Place it under an informative heading, in a table, or in an appendix so it can be inspected without blocking the summary.

Lists are appropriate for parallel facts, options, actions, or risks. Narrative paragraphs are appropriate for causes, reasoning, and chronology. Avoid nested lists when separate headings would make ownership and hierarchy clearer.

## Emphasis

Use headings and placement as the primary emphasis system. Random bold words inside dense paragraphs rarely form a coherent summary. If a phrase deserves emphasis, consider moving its full point into the section opening or executive layer.

## Requests and Next Steps

Make an action operational when the source supports it:

- actor or approver;
- action or decision;
- deadline;
- consequence or dependency;
- what happens after approval.

Never invent an owner or date. A missing owner or deadline belongs in Source gaps or in the memo as an explicit unresolved item when that uncertainty itself needs a decision.
