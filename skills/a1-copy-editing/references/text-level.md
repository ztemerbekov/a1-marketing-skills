# Text Level

At text level, edit the reading path: why the reader should continue, where the main point appears, how sections connect, and whether the structure serves the source-supported job.

## Useful Outcome

The useful outcome answers: why would this reader choose to read this text?

For marketing and editorial copy, the outcome may help a reader understand an offer, compare options, see an important condition, trust a supported claim, learn from an experience, or take a next step already present in the sources.

Do not invent a new audience need or strategic objective. When the source does not establish one, make the existing point easier to find and follow the weak-source rule in [source-boundary.md](source-boundary.md).

## Reader

Edit for the reader implied by the allowed sources and current instruction.

Check whether:

- the text answers the questions that source-supported reader would bring;
- author or company self-presentation displaces reader value;
- terms match the reader's likely level of knowledge;
- important conditions and limitations are visible;
- examples and scenarios are relevant to the selected reader.

When the reader is not specified, use only a conservative inference from the sources. Do not fabricate a persona.

## Main Point

Move the main point early enough for the format and reading mode. Do not begin with company history, a universal observation, or ceremonial setup when the reader first needs the answer, change, offer, or decision.

Useful moves include:

- put the answer or supported conclusion before its chronology;
- place the strongest existing reader value before secondary features;
- group evidence beside the claim it supports;
- separate a decision or action from background detail;
- preserve caution when support is incomplete.

## Reading Mode

Decide whether the source should support selective reading or continuous reading.

For practical, reference, marketing, and workplace text, readers often scan for one answer. Use informative headings, strong opening sentences, lists, tables, captions, and links to make that possible.

For a narrative or chain of reasoning, continuous reading may be necessary. Preserve the sequence when skipping would destroy meaning; do not manufacture suspense in material intended for quick reference.

## Structure

Choose structure from the material, not from a universal formula. Source-supported organizing dimensions may include:

- reader questions;
- situations or eligibility conditions;
- steps or chronology;
- audiences;
- themes;
- options and stable comparison dimensions;
- claim, evidence, counterpoint, and limit;
- problem, response, and result.

You may reorder, group, split, combine, or label existing material when the operation and explicit constraints allow it. Do not create absent marketing blocks, arguments, or examples.

Avoid unnecessary nesting in ordinary short and medium-length text. A title and one visible level of informative section headings is often enough.

## Opening

Treat the opening as content, not ceremony. Strong source-faithful openings may begin with:

- the answer or recommendation;
- a concrete fact;
- the reader's specific situation;
- a source-supported scene or example;
- the event or change;
- a concise map of what follows.

Remove throat-clearing that tells the reader the topic is important without adding information.

## Ending

End when the text fulfills its job. Depending on the source, the ending may contain a next step, decision rule, implication, limitation, contact, or story resolution.

Remove conclusions that only repeat the table of contents, announce that the topic has been covered, or add a generic moral unsupported by the source.

## Completion Check

The text-level edit is complete when the reader can identify the purpose and main point early, follow or scan the material in an appropriate order, and reach an ending that completes the source-supported job.
