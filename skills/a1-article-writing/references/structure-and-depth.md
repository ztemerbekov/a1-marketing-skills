# Structure and Depth

Choose structure from the reader's job, the source material, and the intended reading mode.

## Useful Outcome

The useful outcome answers: why would this reader choose to spend time on this article?

Make it observable. Useful articles help a reader understand a situation, compare options, perform a task, avoid an error, evaluate evidence, or see a credible experience from the inside.

Do not confuse the author's publishing goal with the reader's outcome. `Promote our expertise` is an author goal; `help operations leads recognize when manual reconciliation becomes risky` is a reader outcome.

## Structure Families

Use one family or combine them when the material supports it:

- **Questions:** organize around the real questions readers bring.
- **Sequence:** lead through steps, stages, or chronology when order changes the outcome.
- **Situations:** separate cases, reader conditions, or decision contexts.
- **Comparison:** hold dimensions constant so options can be evaluated fairly.
- **Thematic modules:** group a broad subject into distinct, self-contained areas.
- **Claim and evidence:** state a position, then develop the reasons, examples, counterpoints, and limits.
- **Narrative or case:** follow a person, conflict, decisions, consequences, and resolution.

Avoid deep nesting in ordinary articles. Prefer a title and one visible level of informative section headings unless the material is genuinely book-length.

## Reading Mode

Most practical articles should support selective reading. Put useful information in headings, opening sentences, lists, tables, captions, and links so a reader can locate an answer without reading every paragraph.

Use a continuous narrative when the article must build context, tension, or a chain of reasoning that would break if read out of order. Do not manufacture suspense in a reference article.

## Openings

An opening earns attention by beginning with material rather than ceremony. Suitable openings include:

- the answer or recommendation;
- a concrete fact or finding;
- a source-supported scene or experience;
- the reader's specific situation;
- a precise conflict or question;
- a concise map of what the article will resolve.

Remove universal throat-clearing, broad claims about the modern world, and previews that merely say the topic is important. The opening is part of the article's evidence and voice, not a separate performance.

## Depth

Depth comes from resolution, not length. Develop a claim through the material readers need to recognize and use it:

- a concrete example and relevant counterexample;
- the mechanism: why the result occurs;
- implementation: how to act;
- conditions and limits;
- a comparison against a realistic alternative;
- consequences, costs, or tradeoffs;
- source-supported detail appropriate to the reader's level.

Advice is incomplete when it explains only why something matters. Add how to apply it when the sources support practical guidance.

Examples should let readers rehearse the idea in a recognizable situation. Use supplied examples or verified research; never invent a case that could be mistaken for reported fact.

## Tone

Default to an equal, direct relationship with the reader. Tone comes from stance, empathy, subject knowledge, detail, and channel—not from scattering jokes or branded adjectives.

Preserve a credible author's perspective. Avoid synthetic personas and forced familiarity. When the subject involves constraints, acknowledge the reader's real difficulty instead of turning advice into a moral judgment.

## Endings

End when the article has fulfilled its promise. Depending on the job, the ending may provide:

- a next action;
- a decision rule;
- a concise implication;
- limitations or open questions;
- the resolution of a source-supported story.

Do not repeat the table of contents, announce that the topic has been covered, or add a generic inspirational moral.
