# Release Structure

Use the smallest structure that makes the news easy to understand and reuse.

## News Event and Angle

A release needs a concrete change: something launched, opened, published, agreed, made available, scheduled, completed, or changed for identifiable people.

Choose an angle from reader relevance, not internal effort. `The team worked for six months` is background; `the new service reduces the application steps from six to two` may be news when verified and relevant.

If the source event is weak, state it plainly. Do not compensate with `groundbreaking`, `major`, `unique`, `revolutionary`, or an equivalent superlative.

## Headline

The headline should identify the event and its most useful consequence. Prefer a concrete subject and verb.

Suitable patterns include:

- `[Organization] launches [specific offer] for [audience or use]`
- `[Program or event] opens [specific opportunity] in [place]`
- `[Research owner] publishes [finding or dataset] on [topic]`
- `[Partners] introduce [change] that affects [audience]`

These are patterns, not templates. Fit the headline to the publication and the news.

## Lead

The first paragraph should answer the most relevant parts of:

- what happened;
- who is responsible or affected;
- where and when;
- what changes for the reader or market;
- how to participate or learn more when action is time-sensitive.

Avoid opening with `is pleased to announce`, organizational history, program ceremony, or a spokesperson attribution. Put the event first.

## Body

Give each paragraph one reusable subject. Depending on the event, useful body sections may explain:

- how the product, program, research, or event works;
- eligibility, timing, location, price, or access;
- a verified result, mechanism, or comparison;
- the role of partners or funding;
- what participants or customers can do;
- relevant context or prior milestones.

Explain a technical term in reader language when the source supports the explanation. Keep specialist detail available for trade media without forcing it into the lead.

## Quote

Use an approved quote for perspective that factual prose cannot supply as well: why a choice was made, what the organization commits to, what problem practitioners see, or what the event changes for people.

Omit a quote that only says the speaker is pleased, proud, or confident. Never strengthen or dramatize an approved quote beyond the supplied meaning.

## Action

For an event, application period, program, or public offer, state:

- who can act;
- the exact action;
- deadline, date, time, location, price, and eligibility when relevant;
- the full destination link or contact;
- what happens after registration or inquiry when supplied.

## Boilerplate and Media Contact

Place organization background after the news. Use approved, current facts that help a journalist identify the organization.

End with a media contact and useful assets when supplied:

- name and role;
- email or phone;
- organization site;
- press kit, photography, research, or registration link.

Do not fill missing contacts or boilerplate with plausible information.
